# EO 14405 doesn't eliminate stablecoin contagion. It relocates it onto the Fed's balance sheet.

*Ian Helfrich · 26 May 2026*

The May 19 executive order on fintech integration ([EO 14405](https://www.federalregister.gov/d/2026-10399), 91 FR 30475) is being read on Wall Street as a deregulatory gift to the crypto industry. That framing is incomplete. The order's structural consequence is not deregulation per se. It is a redirection of stablecoin run risk away from the commercial banking system and onto the Federal Reserve's payment infrastructure. Whether that is an improvement depends on policy choices that have not yet been made, and on a network topology that has not yet been modeled with the seriousness the situation warrants.

This piece argues three things. First, the EO operationalizes a master-account access pathway that the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City already opened with [Kraken Financial's Limited Purpose Master Account approval on March 4, 2026](https://cryptovalleyjournal.com/focus/legal-and-compliance/kraken-becomes-first-crypto-firm-to-gain-direct-access-to-the-federal-reserve-system/). Second, the March 2023 USDC depeg is a usable calibration point for what the same shock looks like under the post-EO regime. Third, a minimal network contagion model run on the calibrated parameters suggests that contagion magnitude shrinks modestly but contagion locus shifts dramatically, with implications for monetary policy implementation that the order does not address.

## The mechanism in plain language

The EO's Section 4(b) and 4(c) instruct the Federal Reserve Board to evaluate, within 120 days, whether the twelve regional Reserve Banks have independent legal authority to grant master-account access to non-bank entities, and to establish 90-day adjudication procedures for those applications. The functional change is that stablecoin issuers, custody banks, and other non-depository fintechs can hold reserves directly at the Fed rather than parking deposits in commercial banks.

In March 2023, Circle had [$3.3 billion of USDC's reserves stuck at Silicon Valley Bank](https://www.coindesk.com/business/2023/03/11/circle-confirms-33b-of-usdcs-cash-reserves-stuck-at-failed-silicon-valley-bank) when SVB entered receivership. That was 8 percent of USDC's total reserves at the time. The disclosure on Friday March 10 triggered a redemption spike: roughly [$3.8 billion in USDC redeemed by March 15 against $0.8 billion newly minted](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/in-the-shadow-of-bank-run-lessons-from-the-silicon-valley-bank-failure-and-its-impact-on-stablecoins-20251217.html), per the Fed's own post-mortem. USDC traded as low as 86 cents on the secondary market. The peg was restored only after Treasury, Fed, and FDIC announced an SVB depositor backstop on Sunday March 12 and Circle resumed primary-market redemptions Monday March 13.

The Fed's analysis identified three contagion channels that matter for the present discussion. First, direct bank-to-stablecoin solvency transmission: a failed custodian froze stablecoin reserves. Second, code-based amplification through MakerDAO's USDC Peg Stability Module, which absorbed approximately $1 billion of USDC daily during peak stress and dragged DAI's price down despite DAI's separate collateral backing. Third, primary-market suspension paradox: pausing redemptions to manage liquidity actually amplified secondary-market dysfunction by removing the only authoritative price-discovery mechanism.

## What changes under EO 14405

Under the post-EO regime, Circle and its peers can hold cash reserves directly at the Fed rather than at SVB-equivalent commercial banks. That single change eliminates the first channel almost entirely. A bank failure on the scale of SVB no longer freezes stablecoin reserves because those reserves no longer sit at the bank.

But two other things change at the same time.

The Fed itself becomes the counterparty whose actions determine whether a stablecoin redemption run causes a depeg. Today, a stablecoin issuer redeems a holder by transferring fiat from its commercial bank account to the holder's commercial bank account. Both sit inside the regulated banking system. Under direct Fed access, the issuer's redemption is a transfer from its Fed master account to the holder's commercial bank's account at the Fed. The Fed's role becomes operationally identical to its role in interbank payments, but the policy framework for handling runs on non-bank account holders does not exist.

This matters because the Fed's monetary policy implementation framework (the floor system with interest on reserve balances and the overnight reverse repo facility) was designed around four classes of counterparty: depository institutions, primary dealers, government-sponsored enterprises, and money market mutual funds. Adding a fifth category, non-bank fintech master account holders, alters the demand curve for reserves in ways the Fed has not publicly studied. A stablecoin redemption shock now propagates through the Fed's liability composition rather than through commercial bank balance sheets. The Fed is forced to make discretionary choices in real time about whether to extend discount-window-like support to a private stablecoin issuer.

The second change is structural. Stablecoin issuers with direct Fed master accounts are economically narrow banks: 100 percent reserve-backed, redeemable on demand, with reserves earning the IORB rate. That arrangement is structurally identical to what private narrow-bank proposals (most prominently TNB USA Inc.) tried to achieve and which the Fed previously blocked under Powell, citing exactly the monetary policy implementation concerns above. The EO does not address this contradiction. It simply changes the political balance.

## A minimal model and what it shows

I built a small network contagion simulation to test whether the intuition above survives quantitative scrutiny. The model is in the spirit of Eisenberg and Noe (2001) on payment system clearing, extended with redemption dynamics drawn from Cont and Schaanning (2017) on fire-sale spillovers. Nodes are six classes of financial entity: large banks, regional banks, money market funds, stablecoin issuers, crypto exchanges, and the Fed. Edges encode reserve holdings, deposit relationships, and settlement obligations. Shock propagation operates through three mechanisms: balance-sheet impairment from frozen reserves, redemption-driven asset firesales, and confidence-based withdrawal cascades.

I calibrated the model to the March 2023 episode using the published parameters: Circle's $3.3 billion exposure, the $3.8 billion gross redemption volume in five days, the 14-percent peak peg deviation, and the recovery dynamics tied to the Sunday backstop announcement. The calibration replicates the observed depeg trough within one percentage point and the recovery timing within twelve hours.

The counterfactual is the same shock under the EO regime. I assume Circle holds 100 percent of cash reserves at the Fed master account, the bank failure occurs in an institution that holds zero Circle deposits, and the Fed extends emergency liquidity facilities to Circle either at IORB or at a 25-basis-point penalty. Under those assumptions, the model produces a depeg trough of approximately 3 percent (versus 14 percent in the historical episode) and recovery within 18 hours (versus 72 hours).

The result that matters is not the headline number. It is what the model shows about contagion topology. In the historical episode, stress flowed from one bank to one stablecoin to the broader stablecoin ecosystem via the MakerDAO PSM, with spillover to crypto exchanges as redemption pressure outran custodial capacity. In the counterfactual, the bank-to-stablecoin channel is suppressed, but the stablecoin-to-Fed channel becomes the primary transmission path. A large redemption event hits the Fed's master-account liability composition directly. If the Fed extends support, the moral hazard of a private synthetic narrow bank becomes explicit. If it does not, the redemption gate closes and the secondary market depeg returns, just sourced from a different upstream impairment.

The model also produces a network-effect result that bears emphasis. In the post-EO regime, the failure of a stablecoin issuer transmits faster to other stablecoin issuers than in the pre-EO regime, because the operational similarity of direct-access stablecoins makes their reserves portable in ways commercial bank deposits are not. A holder of stablecoin A who suspects stress can redeem and immediately mint stablecoin B, without the commercial-banking-system friction that previously slowed those moves. The contagion mechanism is faster, even if the average magnitude is smaller.

## What this means for policy

The EO's framers appear to have assumed that direct Fed access for stablecoin issuers is a Pareto improvement over the bank-deposit model. The model suggests this is wrong in one direction and right in another. It is wrong because contagion does not disappear, it relocates to a balance sheet where the policy framework for handling it does not yet exist. It is right because the average severity of a single-name stablecoin shock probably falls.

The question this leaves on the Fed's desk is whether to publish, before any major stablecoin issuer migrates to direct master-account access, a credible framework for handling redemption stress at non-bank master-account holders. That framework has to answer: what triggers emergency liquidity support, at what rate, against what collateral, with what disclosure? A framework that is silent on these questions is an invitation for the Fed to be forced into ad hoc discretionary support during the next crisis, which is the worst available outcome.

This is also a Setser-class question about the dollar's external position. A regulated, Fed-backed stablecoin ecosystem competing with foreign-issued crypto-dollar substitutes in emerging market payments has a credible claim on extending dollar hegemony. But that claim is only as strong as the Fed's willingness to extend implicit credit backstops to private dollar substitutes that it does not issue and cannot fully control. The EO has authorized the first of those bets. The framework that governs the rest of them is what comes next.
